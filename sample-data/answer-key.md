# Answer key

The problems planted in each skill's example, so you can check what Claude finds when you say
"try it". Claude may find a little more than is listed here; a good run finds everything listed.
These answers sit outside the skill folders on purpose, so Claude can't read them.

## 01 Hiring Manager Intake (`hiring-manager-intake`)

Example: intake notes for Team Leader, Customer Operations (four roles).

1. The year-one section is vague asks ("rockstars", "natural leaders", "a good culture fit for the floor", "hit the numbers"), with outcomes nobody could check at twelve months.
2. Must-have 1 asks for at least two years as a people manager, though the job is a first step into management and must-have 3 accepts feedback experience as a senior adviser, buddy or trainer.
3. Must-have 4 caps sick days for internal applicants (a disability discrimination risk) and plans to ask external candidates about sickness at first interview (a pre-offer health question under section 60).
4. A decision is already made: one role is "already spoken for" by the acting team leader on Team 6, who skips the coaching exercise and gets "a quick chat".

## 02 Role Success Profile (`role-success-profile`)

Example: a draft profile for Smart-Meter Engineer (12 roles).

1. Criterion 1 is traits ("passionate about the energy transition", "a resilient self-starter").
2. Criteria 3 and 4 duplicate each other: explaining the meter clearly and "excellent communication skills with customers" would be assessed on the same evidence.
3. Criterion 6, "will stay with Brackenholt for at least five years", can't be tested before hiring, yet it's down to be assessed at interview.
4. The safety-critical work in the job summary (isolating the supply, the gas tightness test, stopping and reporting danger) has no criterion or check.

## 03 Essential Criteria Review (`essential-criteria-review`)

Example: the person specification for Finance Business Partner.

1. A 2:1 degree in accounting, finance or economics on top of a professional qualification the work already requires; it can disadvantage older applicants.
2. At least 10 years' post-qualification experience, which tracks age.
3. "Native English speaker, for writing board papers": a race (national origin) risk; state the level of written English the work needs.
4. A full UK driving licence for a hybrid Sheffield office role with no driving: a disability risk with no job reason.

(The professional accounting qualification is a control: it stays, with a justification beside it.)

## 04 Interview Plan (`interview-plan`)

Example: an interview plan for Software Developer (two remote roles).

1. Criterion D, explaining technical choices to non-technical colleagues, is assessed at no stage.
2. Criterion B, code review, is a skill tested only by a knowledge question; it needs a work sample.
3. Candidates with a strong GitHub profile or a developer referral skip the take-home, breaking same-for-everyone, and stage 3 then asks them to extend a take-home they never did.
4. Stage 4 is an informal "culture fit chat" with no set questions, no criterion and one assessor.

## 05 Structured Interview Guide (`structured-interview-guide`)

Example: a 30-minute first interview guide for Customer Adviser.

1. Q2 has a swap-in question for candidates who've worked in a contact centre, so candidates get different questions.
2. The timings add up to 41 minutes in a 30-minute slot.
3. Q7, "Where do you see yourself in five years?", tests none of the four criteria.
4. "Add a curveball scenario of your own" invites unplanned hypotheticals.

## 06 Situational Questions (`situational-questions`)

Example: a field supervisor's draft scenarios for Smart-Meter Engineer.

1. S3 ("A customer asks you to skip the safety checks... Would you do it?") has no real dilemma and gives the answer away.
2. S4 relies on insider jargon ("JobPad shows an Amber", "ring the Hub") that external candidates can't know.
3. S5 has no strong-answer points: "you'll know a good answer when you hear it".
4. S6 is labelled "Culture fit", asks about joining the team at the pub, tests no criterion and rewards socialising.

## 07 Work Sample Task (`work-sample-task`)

Example: a take-home task for Software Developer.

1. The task is the next item on the billing backlog: real work the company would use.
2. No time limit, nothing on unpaid time, and extra effort is rewarded ("most strong candidates spend a weekend on it").
3. The marking guide scores "Overall impression", "Would I enjoy working with this person?" and a bonus for anything that impresses, none tied to the three criteria.
4. No live follow-up: the candidate never explains their choices.

## 08 Question Fairness Check (`question-fairness-check`)

Example: a question set for Team Leader, Customer Operations.

1. Q3 asks about time off sick in the last two years: a health question before an offer (section 60).
2. Q4 is leading ("You'd be comfortable... wouldn't you?").
3. Q6 uses insider jargon ("the Pulse board is red", "a Huddle 3") that favours internal candidates.
4. Q7 asks "Have you got childcare sorted?": a family and caring question with a sex discrimination risk. Ask every candidate whether they can work the shift pattern.

## 09 Scoring Guide (`scoring-guide`)

Example: a scoring guide for Team Leader, Customer Operations.

1. "Planning the team's day" has vague anchors ("Excellent planning", "Good planning, with some gaps", "Poor planning").
2. The top anchor for "Handling a difficult conversation" rewards style ("confident, articulate and polished", "real presence").
3. "Culture fit" is a criterion, with anchors that reward insider knowledge and shared background ("Feels like one of us already").
4. Criterion 4, dealing with escalated complaints, has no question and no scale.

## 10 Interviewer Note Sheet (`interviewer-note-sheet`)

Example: a note sheet for Smart-Meter Engineer interviews.

1. It tells interviewers the notes are never shown to candidates, so "write freely". Candidates can request them under a subject access request.
2. A "First impressions" box asks about appearance and accent.
3. Question 3 asks interviewers to note age, old injuries, health conditions and sickness absence: section 60 and age, with no evidence box.
4. An "Overall impression and gut feel" box ("would you want this person on your patch?").

## 11 Interviewer Briefing (`interviewer-briefing`)

Example: the panel briefing for Finance Business Partner.

1. A director's question about days off sick is called "fine to keep" (section 60), and the questions-to-avoid list leaves out health and disability.
2. The adjustments section gives a candidate's diagnoses and medication, and adds an assumption ("be patient if they seem nervous"). The panel needs only the adjustments.
3. The director gives their view first and the panel agrees scores together: nobody scores alone first, and the senior view anchors everyone.
4. Notes and scores are to be posted in a leadership Teams channel wider than the panel.

## 12 Interviewer Practice Session (`interviewer-practice-session`)

Example: a TA partner's rough notes for a session before the Customer Adviser intake. Draft-only skill: Claude lists these, then drafts with each one fixed.

1. It plans to replay a real candidate's recorded interview: personal data reused without telling them. Use fictional answers.
2. Scores are called out and agreed together, so nobody scores alone in writing first.
3. A "reading people" slot teaches trusting your gut in the first two minutes.
4. The plan adds up to 75 minutes in a one-hour slot, with only 10 minutes on scoring.

## 13 Interview Invitation (`interview-invitation`)

Example: a second-stage invitation for Smart-Meter Engineer.

1. Too little notice: sent on a Thursday afternoon for the following Monday morning.
2. Jargon ("Stage 2 AC", "a PA followed by a CBI with the FS and a TAP").
3. Missing practical details the plan gives: length and finish time, the address and which entrance, step-free access and parking, and the closed-toe shoes needed.
4. A health question (conditions, injuries, two years' sickness) in place of a plain line asking what would help (section 60).

## 14 Advance Questions Sheet (`advance-questions-sheet`)

Example: a sheet and sending plan for Team Leader, Customer Operations.

1. Internal candidates get it on a Teams channel a week ahead; external candidates only if they ask for an adjustment.
2. Those external candidates get it at 16:00 the working day before their interview.
3. The scoring guide leaks: a "What the panel will score highly" list sits under each question.
4. Question 2 uses an in-house scorecard name external candidates can't know, and no longer matches the guide's wording.

## 15 Adjustment Request Reply (`adjustment-request-reply`)

Example: a draft reply to a Customer Adviser candidate.

1. It asks for the condition and a GP or specialist letter, and won't consider the request until they arrive.
2. The request for written questions is left vague ("will see what they can do on the day").
3. It refuses another way to do the group exercise without discussion or alternatives.
4. It forwards the candidate's whole email to all three panel members.

## 16 Candidate AI Use Statement (`candidate-ai-use-statement`)

Example: a draft statement for Software Developer candidates.

1. A blanket ban on AI "of any kind... at any stage" that contradicts the take-home rules, bans CV polishing and catches accessibility tools with no exception for adjustments.
2. It claims detection technology (eye movement, background audio, pasted code) that isn't used.
3. Threats ("immediately", a possible bar from future roles, "We will know") with no chance to explain.
4. It doesn't disclose the ATS's AI CV summary and match score that hiring managers see, and has no privacy notice link or line saying people decide.

## 17 Interview Integrity Plan (`interview-integrity-plan`)

Example: an integrity plan for remote Software Developer interviews.

1. An "attention monitor" flags candidates by eye movement, which can disadvantage disabled and neurodivergent candidates.
2. Camera on "with no exceptions", cutting across adjustments.
3. Extra ID checks and a room scan only for candidates whose CV shows study or work outside the UK, without telling them: inconsistent, covert and a race discrimination risk.
4. The concern process tells candidates they've been "caught" and rejects them as fraud, with no fact-finding, no chance to respond and no named decision-maker.

## 18 Debrief Agenda (`debrief-agenda`)

Example: a debrief agenda for Team Leader, Customer Operations.

1. A "first impressions" round before scores are in, and scores entered the day after the meeting.
2. The hiring manager speaks first on every candidate, and discussion runs candidate by candidate rather than criterion by criterion.
3. Only internal candidates are discussed using performance ratings and attendance records, so the evidence differs, and attendance can bring in health or disability.
4. An "overall feel" and "culture fit" show of hands, with only Appoint or Reject recorded and no reasons or named recorder.

## 19 Decision Record (`decision-record`)

Example: a draft decision record for Finance Business Partner, Candidate C.

1. An opinion with no evidence ("came across as quite arrogant").
2. The reasons don't match the scores: they blame influencing, which scored 4, and never mention forecasting and modelling, which scored 2 and failed the rule.
3. An unrelated remark about the candidate's commute from Hull.
4. The adjustment is recorded with a diagnosis instead of what was done.

## 20 Reference Request (`reference-request`)

Example: a reference email and form for Smart-Meter Engineer.

1. No consent, and sent before any offer, to employers taken from the application history.
2. A personality question and a 1 to 10 attitude rating.
3. A family commitments question about the on-call rota (sex and caring risk); ask every candidate directly.
4. Health and sickness questions before an offer (section 60).

## 21 Candidate Feedback Note (`candidate-feedback-note`)

Example: a hiring manager's draft feedback to an internal Team Leader candidate.

1. Vague: "weren't quite the right fit for the team leader group at this stage".
2. A comparison with the successful candidates.
3. It contradicts the notes: its development point is coaching (scored 4) and it never mentions performance and attendance (scored 2, the recorded reason).
4. A promise about future roles ("you'll walk straight into one").

## 22 Not Progressing Email (`not-progressing-email`)

Example: the Customer Adviser ATS set-up and templates.

1. Late or missing messages: nothing after the final assessment ("assume you have been unsuccessful" after three weeks), and screening rejections batched monthly.
2. Cold template language ("Dear Applicant", "We regret to inform you", a no-reply sender).
3. Reasons that could be discriminatory: full-time availability for a role advertised part-time too, and "a recent, unbroken work history".
4. "We will keep your CV on file for 12 months" with no consent.

## 23 Candidate Experience Survey (`candidate-experience-survey`)

Example: a survey for Smart-Meter Engineer candidates.

1. Sent only to the 12 successful candidates, on a tablet at induction, missing the 49 others and not anonymous in practice.
2. Marketing and leading questions ("our friendly, well-organised assessment day", whether they'd choose Brackenholt as their supplier).
3. Questions that identify the respondent (session date, the named assessor).
4. It asks for the condition or disability behind an adjustment.

## 24 Hiring Round Review (`hiring-round-review`)

Example: a round review for Software Developer.

1. Interviewers are named in the scoring table and text.
2. Monitoring data (gender, age band, ethnicity) is joined to individual finalists' scores and outcomes.
3. A conclusion from tiny numbers ("1 of 3 women against 4 of 9 men") drives the first change for next round.
4. It claims every candidate got an answer, but the table shows 9 of 64 screening rejections were never told.
