# Interview Integrity Plan

Sets out the checks a hiring process uses against stand-in candidates and live AI help, stage by stage, and what happens if an interviewer has a concern. Or checks a plan someone has already written. Most of the protection sits in how the interview is designed, so the plan stays fair to every candidate and hiring managers know what to do when something doesn't add up.

## When to use

- "We think someone else turned up to a second interview. How do we stop it happening again?"
- "Candidates for our developer roles seem to be reading AI answers on video calls. What's fair to do about it?"
- "Should we check ID at the start of video interviews?"
- "A vendor wants to sell us software that flags cheating. Should we use it?"
- "Check our interview integrity guidance before it goes to hiring managers."

Wrong tool for deciding whether a particular candidate cheated: a named person decides that after the process in this plan. To tell candidates what AI use you allow, use Candidate AI Use Statement; for a task that's hard to fake, use Work Sample Task.

## Inputs

Ask once for anything missing. Defaults in brackets.

1. **Essential:** the role and each interview stage, with its format (video, phone, in person, take-home or live task). [a video first interview and a video final interview]
2. **Essential:** what worries you, in general terms: stand-ins, live AI help or both, and whether something has already happened. [both; no incident yet]
3. What candidates are told about AI use, if you have a statement. [preparing with AI is fine; live AI help during the interview isn't]
4. Your identity and right to work process: when ID is seen, by whom and what's kept. [the recruitment coordinator sees ID at the right to work check after a conditional offer, following Home Office guidance]
5. Whether the role justifies an in-person stage, and why. [only where the job is site-based or needs access to sensitive systems from day one]
6. Tools in use: video platform features, recording, any monitoring software. [the video platform only; no recording]

Never ask for a candidate's name, appearance, nationality, immigration status, health or any protected characteristic. If the user describes a concern about one candidate, call them Candidate A, keep to what was said or shown, and say you've done so.

## Steps

1. Read what the user has given, including any policy, invitation or AI use statement. If they've given an existing plan or guidance, this is a check job: run the Quality checks on it, list every problem with where it sits and why it matters, then write a corrected plan.
2. Ask for missing essentials once, in one message, five questions at most, with the defaults shown. If the user says "just draft it", use the defaults and list them as assumptions.
3. Map the risk stage by stage. For each stage, write what a stand-in or live AI help could do there, and what in the interview design already makes it hard. Keep the checks proportionate to the role: a high-volume first interview needs fewer than a remote technical role with system access. Every check applies to every candidate at that stage.
4. Set the identity step. At the start of the first live interview, the interviewer or coordinator confirms identity in line with policy, for example with the candidate holding photo ID to the camera. Record only that ID was seen and matched, unless your policy and privacy notice say to keep more. At the right to work check, which Home Office guidance expects to be done the same way for every person before they start, confirm the person checked is the person interviewed.
5. Set the camera rule: camera on for video interviews, for everyone, stated in the invitation, unless an agreed adjustment says otherwise. Where an adjustment applies (camera off, captions, breaks, extra time, questions in advance, a screen reader), adapt the check, for example by confirming identity in a short separate call. The adjustment always stands, and taking one up is never treated as a warning sign.
6. Build integrity into the questions. It's the fairest check, and it keeps the method that works: Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens (2022) found structured interviews the top-ranked selection procedure for predicting job performance. Add:
   - planned probes that ask candidates to go deeper into their own answer ("What did you do next?", "What would you change if you did it again?", "What did the numbers show?");
   - questions that ask candidates to explain their own work: their take-home task, a decision on their CV, a piece of work they bring;
   - for technical roles, a short live extension of the candidate's own submitted work.
   Write the probes into the interview guide so every candidate gets the same ones. That keeps the structure Campion, Palmer and Campion (1997) describe: the same questions for everyone, with limited, planned prompting.
7. Add an in-person stage only where the role justifies it. Write the reason down, apply it to everyone at that stage, and offer travel costs and adjustments.
8. List what the plan leaves out. No tool or rule that infers cheating from eye movement, gaze, typing rhythm, facial expression or behaviour: these can disadvantage disabled and neurodivergent candidates, and anyone who looks away to think or reads captions. No covert checks: nothing candidates haven't been told about in advance, and nothing done to some candidates and not others. If a monitoring tool is already in use, point to DSIT's Responsible AI in Recruitment guide: check the tool against reasonable adjustments, consider whether it's automated decision-making under UK GDPR Article 22, and run a DPIA.
9. Write the concern process, with an owner role for each step:
   1. During the interview, the interviewer carries on as normal and courteously. They never accuse or challenge the candidate.
   2. Straight after, they note what was said or shown, factually, with times. A concern rests on facts such as an ID mismatch, a second voice on the call, or being unable to explain their own submitted work. Pauses, looking away, reading notes, a speech pattern or a camera fault are never grounds on their own.
   3. The TA partner gathers the facts, including whether an adjustment explains what was seen.
   4. The candidate gets a chance to respond: a neutral invitation to a short follow-up conversation that goes over that part of the interview again, with an identity check if identity is the concern.
   5. A named role decides on the facts and the candidate's response, records the reasons, and takes HR advice before withdrawing an application or offer.
10. Write the paragraph for the invitation that tells every candidate which checks happen and how to ask for an adjustment, and the neutral follow-up message for step 9.4.
11. Run the Quality checks and fix what fails before showing anything.
12. Present the plan, then **Assumptions** and **Check before you use it**: your ID and right to work process, that your privacy notice covers the identity step and any recording, your video platform's settings, and who in your organisation decides on a concern.
13. Offer a .docx, a one-page version for interviewers, or the Candidate AI Use Statement, Interview Invitation or Work Sample Task skills.

## Output format

**Interview integrity plan: [role]**
Prepared by [role], [date]. Applies to every candidate at every stage below.

1. **What we're guarding against**: two or three lines, in general terms.
2. **Checks by stage**

| Stage and format | Check | Who | What's recorded | How candidates are told |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

3. **Built into the interview**

| Criterion or question | Planned probe | Explain your own work |
| --- | --- | --- |

4. **When an adjustment applies**

| Check | How it adapts |
| --- | --- |

5. **What we don't use**: three to five bullets.
6. **If a concern comes up**: the five steps, each with an owner role.
7. **Wording for candidates**: the invitation paragraph and the follow-up message.

One page, plus the two pieces of wording.

## Quality checks

- Every check applies to every candidate at that stage and is told to candidates in advance; no check is hidden, and none is aimed at some candidates or groups.
- The identity step happens at the start of the first live interview, records only what policy allows, and is matched at a right to work check done the same way for everyone.
- Every check says how it adapts when an adjustment applies, and nothing treats an adjustment, a camera fault, pauses or looking away as a sign of cheating.
- No tool, rule or interviewer instruction infers cheating from eye movement, gaze, facial expression or behaviour.
- Every probe asks candidates to go deeper into their own answer or explain their own work, and the probes are written into the guide for everyone.
- The concern process runs in order (facts gathered, the candidate given a chance to respond, a named role decides and records reasons), and no wording accuses or presumes guilt ("caught", "cheat", "fraud", "fake").
- Any in-person stage has a written job reason and applies to everyone at that stage.

## Guardrails

- The one rule for this pack: Claude drafts the questions, guides and letters. Interviewers gather the evidence, and people make every hiring decision. This plan sets checks and a process. It never judges whether a candidate cheated or was who they said they were; if asked, say that's for the named decision-maker after the concern process, and help gather the facts.
- Personal data: work with roles and candidate letters. Identity checks and interview notes are personal data under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, so keep only what the plan needs and your privacy notice covers. Never paste ID documents, photos or recordings into Claude, and follow your organisation's AI policy.
- Fairness: the same checks for everyone at a stage. A check that puts people with a protected characteristic at a disadvantage, such as disabled or neurodivergent candidates or people with a stammer, needs a job reason and must be proportionate, or it risks indirect discrimination. Never pick people for extra checks by nationality, name, accent, where they studied or how they look; that risks direct discrimination. Never infer a protected characteristic.
- Law: the Equality Act 2010 (direct and indirect discrimination, and the section 20 duty to make reasonable adjustments), the EHRC Employment Statutory Code of Practice, UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 (including Article 22 on automated decisions), ICO guidance on recruitment, Home Office right to work guidance and DSIT's Responsible AI in Recruitment guide. Northern Ireland has its own equality law. Check the current versions. This is a draft plan, never legal advice; take advice before withdrawing an application or offer over a concern.
- No invented facts: policies, tools, stages and dates come from the user or stay as placeholders.
